# What a Sanketana course is

Read this before writing a spine or a lesson. It is the part of the school that is not in the folder structure.

## Who we teach
Students aged 8–18, one-to-one, live, online, across nine countries. Parents are discerning: they can tell
depth from gamified ed-tech and are paying for the former. A course must survive a parent reading it cold.
Positioning is premium and understated. "Coding, taught like a craft." Coach, not curriculum.

## What we're actually teaching
**Code is the artifact; thinking is the skill.** A student comes away with projects they built, but what we are
actually teaching is how they think while building — and the teacher steers that in the moment, one-to-one.
Don't lecture it in the prose; build it into the work.

## How we teach
**Predict → Observe → Explain.** The default shape for any new concept: the student predicts what code will do,
runs it, and explains the gap. Lessons should contain at least one explicit prediction moment; `predict`-type
`predict`-type assessment questions and `predict_*` code files exist for this.

Other habits a lesson should embody:
- Struggle is designed in. A lesson that never lets the student be wrong teaches nothing.
- Teach-back. Ask the student to explain the idea to the teacher, a parent, a younger sibling.
- Real detail, honest imperfection. Show a bug, a wrong first attempt, a limitation.
- One-to-one means the teacher adapts. Teacher notes say where to speed up or slow down; they don't script every word.

## The shape of an hour (60-minute default)
Recap and homework back · teach the new idea · Predict → Observe → Explain · build · reflection · brief the
new homework. Roughly 8 · 10 · 12 · 20 · 5 · 5 minutes. Every lesson in every track runs these six phases,
so a teacher knows the shape before they know the topic. The actual hour is mapped per lesson in
`lesson-plan.md` §5, which also names the one phase to protect when time runs short.

Homework is one focused task plus one stretch, never a worksheet — and it is briefed in class, not left as
a link at the end.

A 1:1 hour with a fast student runs out of lesson before it runs out of time. Every lesson carries a
`practice.md` with two or three projects harder than the classwork, so the answer to "I'm done" is never
"try something".

## Assessment
Three per course, not one per lesson: two formative checks partway through and one summative at the end.
A quiz after every session teaches students to answer questions about the last hour. The formatives exist
to tell the teacher what to re-teach; treat a wrong answer as information about the lesson, not the child.

## Voice
Specific over grand. Real detail with honest imperfection. Confident, not loud. Respects the reader.
Voice, not costume.

Never use: fun, exciting, future-ready, 21st-century, AI-powered, unlock, journey, empower, supercharge,
game-changer. Don't address students as "kids". Don't promise outcomes we can't see.

Write to the student directly in `concepts.md` ("you'll see this again in…"), to the teacher in
`lesson-plan.md` ("watch for students who…"), and to the parent in `curriculum.md`'s parent-safe
sections, which is where every parent-facing view is generated from. The banned words above apply
hardest there — it is the copy with something to sell.

## Ethics is woven, not bolted on
Where a lesson touches data, automation, AI output, or other people, add one question in the Reflection
section. Not a separate unit. Not every lesson.
